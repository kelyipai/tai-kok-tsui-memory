# Faded Borders — 褪邊

## A Visual Philosophy for Tai Kok Tsui Memory

**Faded Borders** treats memory as a physical artifact: every image is a photograph peeled from a family album, every text block is a clipping rescued from a damp newspaper, and every margin carries the stain of time. The philosophy insists that nostalgia is not a filter applied at the end, but a structural principle built into space, material, and rhythm.

### Space and Form
Compositions are organized like a scrapbook laid open on a linoleum table. Elements do not float; they rest, overlap, and tuck under one another with the gravity of real paper. generous margins separate groups, yet within each group items sit close enough to suggest they were collected by the same hand. Corners are slightly softened, edges are allowed to wear, and nothing is perfectly aligned — the meticulously crafted imperfection of a table that has been used for decades.

### Color and Material
The palette is extracted from old Hong Kong: the cream of sun-bleached letterheads, the tobacco brown of wooden shop signs, the muted red of firecracker wrappers, the iron-grey of factory gates, and the pale yellow of aging cellophane. Color is never flat; it carries texture — paper grain, ink bleed, water stains, and the soft bloom of expired film. The result must look as though it was printed in Mong Kok in 1978 and left in a drawer until today.

### Scale and Rhythm
Headlines occupy space with the confidence of hand-painted signage, while captions remain small and intimate, like handwriting on the back of a photograph. Scale shifts create a walking rhythm: a large archival image, a tight cluster of three small prints, a wide band of text, then a single isolated detail. This rhythm mirrors the experience of strolling through an old neighbourhood where empty lots and crowded street fronts alternate without warning.

### Composition and Balance
Balance is achieved through asymmetry and counterweight. A heavy block of sepia on the left is answered by a vertical strip of red labels on the right. A full-bleed photograph is interrupted by a narrow column of white space. Every composition is labored over until it feels both accidental and inevitable — the product of deep expertise in making order feel like memory.

### Visual Hierarchy
Information is encoded through material, not just size. The most important stories are printed on the most worn paper; the most precious photographs have the most faded borders. Typography serves the artifact: small sans-serif labels for facts, a compressed serif for headlines, and occasional brush-script for place names. Text is sparse and essential — never a paragraph where a caption will do, never a caption where a date and a name are enough.

### The Craftsman's Standard
The final work must appear as though countless hours were spent selecting, cropping, toning, and arranging each piece. Every stain, every torn edge, every imperfect alignment is a deliberate choice made by someone at the absolute top of their field. The result is not decoration; it is a meticulously crafted memorial to a place that is still disappearing.
